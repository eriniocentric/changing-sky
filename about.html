About Changing Sky

A community explorer of public alerts from the NSF–DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory. Not an official Rubin Observatory product.

What is this?

Every night the Vera C. Rubin Observatory in Chile photographs the southern sky and compares each new image with earlier ones. Anything that changed — a star that brightened, a galaxy whose core flared, a new supernova, an object no catalogue has seen — produces an alert. Rubin will issue up to millions of alerts every night for ten years, far more than professional astronomers can look at.

Changing Sky puts those alerts on an interactive map of the sky, sorted by what they probably are, with the surrounding sky from many telescopes and wavelengths. Anyone can explore, and signed-in visitors can help decide which alerts deserve a closer look.

How to explore

  1. Pick an alert type on the right (supernovae, variable stars, quasars, the unidentified…). Markers appear on the sky.
  2. Pick a sky survey on the left to change the background: visible light, infrared, ultraviolet, X-ray, radio or gamma rays.
  3. Click a marker to see the alert: the new image, the earlier template, the difference between them (red = brighter now, blue = fainter), and the light curve showing how its brightness has changed.

How voting and follow-up work

  • Sign in (Google or an emailed link) and press ▲ Upvote on alerts you think deserve attention. Add a reason — rising fast, no host galaxy, odd colour, unusual light curve — so scientists know why.
  • If you think an alert is fake (a smudge, a subtraction error, a satellite glint), choose “Possible artefact”. Spotting fakes is just as valuable as spotting discoveries.
  • The Most wanted tab ranks alerts by votes. Voted alerts glow on the sky as Community picks.
  • A moderator checks the top alerts before anything is sent onward — for example a report to the Transient Name Server, observations with education telescopes such as Las Cumbres Observatory, or variable-star observers at AAVSO. Popular is not the same as real, so a person always checks first. The Follow-up tab shows what was chosen and what happened.

Community guidelines

  • Be kind and curious. Everyone here is learning — beginners and professionals alike. Questions are welcome.
  • Say what you see. The best comments describe evidence: “brightened 1 magnitude in 3 days”, “sits right on the galaxy's core”, “looks like a dipole artefact”.
  • Vote honestly. One vote per person per alert. Don't coordinate votes or use several accounts.
  • No personal information in comments — yours or anyone else's.
  • Stay on topic: the alert, the sky, the science. No advertising or spam.
  • Use report on comments that break these rules. Moderators may hide such comments.

Where the data come from

  • Alerts: NSF–DOE Vera C. Rubin Observatory, Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) — world-public prompt alert data, retrieved through the Fink community broker.
  • Sky maps: Aladin Lite and HiPS surveys from CDS Strasbourg and partners (DSS, DESI Legacy Surveys, DES, Pan-STARRS, 2MASS, WISE, GALEX, ROSAT, ASKAP, NVSS, Fermi and Rubin First Look).
  • Classifications: cross-matches with SIMBAD, ESA Gaia DR3, the Milliquas quasar catalogue, VSX and the Transient Name Server, via the CDS XMatch service.
  • Satellite tracks: predicted from public orbital elements (Space-Track.org) — see the satellite layer on the map.

Current data: the night of 14 July 2026 (UTC), the most recent night before Rubin paused operations after a severe winter storm. New nights will be added when alerts resume. Read the privacy policy.
